The definition

Sovereignty is being able to move.

A sovereign website is a website you own and can move. Sovereignty is not where your data is hosted. It is whether you can move it: your site, its data and its schema, to anywhere else, on your own terms.

The definition, in seven principles. This page explains the idea. The seven principles turn it into tests anyone can run against a site, and the maturity model says how far a site has come, from level 0, rented, to level 5, sovereign and proven. Both are proposed, version 0.1, open for comment and licensed CC BY 4.0.

One proprietary part is enough

Owning a site is the easy part; moving it is where it fails. Once there is a piece of proprietary technology in the middle, however small, you cannot move the site, and nobody else can move it for you without rebuilding that part first. You cannot even pay your way out. A paid plugin, a platform's form handler, a hosting company's special function or an agency's private framework is enough.

Lock-in hides in more than one place: where the domain is registered, the technology underneath, and the platform it runs on. A site portable in its code but registered in someone else's name is not sovereign; nor is one in your name that only runs on one company's servers. That is why the principles start with owning the domain and include nothing proprietary in the middle.

Where it is hosted is not sovereignty

Most talk about digital sovereignty is about location: data kept in a data centre in the right country, a supplier incorporated at home. That matters, and it is not enough. A site hosted in the right place, on a platform whose terms say you cannot take it with you, is not yours, wherever the servers are.

You do not have sovereignty if you cannot move your data, your site and its schema somewhere else; if the provider's terms decide what you may do; or if the company can be bought, and its terms and its jurisdiction change with it. A company that is British today can have owners, investors and a jurisdiction elsewhere tomorrow. Squarespace, for example, was taken private by an investment firm in 2024.

The four tests

The four tests are the short form, written for the pattern; the seven principles are the fuller version, with how to test each one. A site that fails any of these four is not sovereign.

  1. 1. Can you move it?

    The site is static files in your own repository, with their history, and can be served from any host. Nothing about it is tied to the people who built it.

  2. 2. Do you own the schema?

    The structure of the site and its content are files in your repository, in open formats, changed by your own AI. An export in a format the vendor chose is access, not control.

  3. 3. Who sets the terms?

    You do: no platform and no subscription in the way, and open-source code that nobody can withdraw.

  4. 4. What if they are bought?

    Nothing changes for you, because you already hold everything. Whoever built or hosts the site can change owners; your repository does not move with them.

The difference is that you can leave

Many website platforms earn every month because you stay. Wix's help page says its sites "need to be hosted and operated on Wix's servers". Squarespace's only export is a WordPress-format file covering pages, one blog and their text and images, and leaving the rest behind. None of this is hidden; it is the business model. These positions are from the platforms' own pages and standard terms, as read in November 2026; see no lock-in for the sources.

A sovereign agentic website is the opposite design: you can leave, with your site, whenever you want, and owe nobody anything.

Why it has to be open

Only open source makes independence possible: if you are suddenly cut off from a service, at least you have a chance. That is why this is an open pattern, which anyone can use without paying us. A site you own should not depend on us existing.

Not a legal or certification claim. Sovereign, here, means owning your site and being able to move it, as defined on this page. It does not certify compliance with any law, standard or regulation, and it is not a guarantee: owning the files means you can move; it does not move them for you.
